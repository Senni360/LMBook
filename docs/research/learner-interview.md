# A candidate conversation before creating an episode

Research prototype · 2026-09-14 · Not a production system prompt or an implemented feature.

This candidate develops the owner's endorsed five areas. The [research findings](learning-experience-findings.md) explain the recommendation, its alternatives, and the evidence limits. The examples below are constructed conversations, not recordings of learner trials.

## What the conversation should accomplish

The learner and SenniBook should finish with the same understanding of what the episode must do. Completing five answers is insufficient if “everything” still means different things to each of them. Equally, the learner does not have to demonstrate mastery or specify an instructional theory before receiving help.

The five areas remain required. Ask the five opening questions in order by default, acknowledging answers already given. If an answer has already established another area, present that understanding for confirmation rather than requiring it to be retyped. Resolving a contradiction can interrupt the sequence; there is no value in continuing with questions that depend on an interpretation the learner has rejected.

Before asking, inspect the material that is actually available. Describe identified sources and possible requirements provisionally. A title, a selected excerpt, or an incomplete extraction does not establish what the entire document contains. The source can answer factual questions about its contents; the learner decides what they need from it.

Suggested opening:

> Let's establish what you need this episode to do. We'll cover five things, then follow up where an answer could change what we make. You can say “I'm not sure”; I'll help you choose.

## The five opening questions

### 1. Intended outcome

> What would you like to be able to understand, explain, or do after listening?

The answer selects the intended activity: exploration, explaining required ideas, following original text, working out a numerical answer, or a combination. The subject name alone is insufficient.

If the answer is “learn about GDP,” a useful follow-up is:

> Is the main goal to explain what GDP means, work out answers to GDP questions, or both?

Use this follow-up only if the answer has not already settled it. An exploratory learner need not invent an exam objective. “Understand the main disagreements well enough to follow the news” is a usable outcome.

### 2. Required coverage

> What needs to be included? If you have a list of required topics or learning goals, tell me which file it is.

The answer establishes what may not be omitted and which material determines that obligation. If a document appears to be a requirements list, offer that identification for confirmation. Do not make the learner transcribe an available list.

“Everything in the file” is a coverage answer. It does not yet authorize paraphrasing or establish an original-word reading. “Only the required terms” needs a check when the actual requirements also ask for explanations or applications.

For an interest-led episode, the answer can be a chosen question or scope with no external requirements. Record that explicitly rather than inventing a syllabus.

### 3. Treatment of the source

> Should we explain the material in our own words, read the original wording with explanatory pauses, or use a mixture?

The learner need not know the term “fidelity.” If they choose a mixture, identify which material needs which treatment. If they want every idea explained, confirm that this means full coverage of the requested ideas with freedom to rephrase. If they want an annotated reading, preserve the selected original text and distinguish commentary from it.

A useful clarification for “don't leave anything out” is:

> Do you mean every idea needs to be explained, or that you also need to hear the original wording?

Ask about commentary language if the source and requested listening language could differ. Translation, explanation, and reading the original are separate choices. Ask where additional context may come from when the requested explanation would need information beyond the supplied material.

### 4. Starting knowledge and ability

> What can you already explain or work out without help?

“I know the definitions, but I don't know how to calculate it” is already valuable information. Do not make the learner prove that difficulty. Knowing a topic name, recognizing a solution, and producing a solution independently are different observations.

If a consequential uncertainty remains, offer one small check drawn from the actual task. For example:

> Would looking at one typical question help us find where to start? You can tell me which part you would try first; you don't need to finish it.

A check is assistance, not an admission test. “I don't know where to begin” can justify starting with a complete worked example. A correct answer to one item does not establish general mastery, and an incorrect answer does not erase relevant knowledge the learner has demonstrated elsewhere.

### 5. Needed assistance

> What tends to trip you up, or what would you like us to spend extra time on?

Use the answer to recommend support. The learner should not need to choose between teaching theories. Relevant difficulties might be selecting a method, keeping track of quantities, following a timeline, understanding why a quotation matters, or connecting ideas that seem unrelated.

If an earlier answer already identifies the difficulty, acknowledge it and use this area to propose help and establish any consequential circumstances. For example: “You've said choosing the quantities is the difficult part. I suggest we work through how to identify them in a question. Will you need to follow this entirely by listening, or would a saved calculation also be useful?” Do not ask the learner to describe the same obstacle twice just to preserve five separate answers.

If the learner cannot identify a difficulty, propose a concrete starting approach using the preceding answers: “I'll explain how to recognize the question type, then work through one example slowly.” Make that proposal easy to correct.

Listening circumstances belong here when they change the support: a learner who can occasionally look at a phone may use a saved equation; someone who wants to keep listening without looking needs a self-contained spoken explanation. Do not ask every learner about every possible visual, animation, pause, or exercise.

## When a follow-up earns its place

Before adding a question, be able to complete this sentence: “The answer could change ___ in the episode or expose ___ that we cannot yet promise.” If there is no concrete completion, do not ask it now.

| Trigger | Decision still missing | Example follow-up or action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Cover everything” | Coverage versus original wording | Confirm whether every idea or every original word must be retained. |
| A course pack and a separate objectives file | Which document determines required learning | Offer the apparent roles for confirmation, with the actual document names. |
| Definitions are familiar; calculations are difficult | Where numerical assistance should begin | Use the reported difficulty; inspect a representative task if method choice remains unclear. |
| “Make it short” plus complete annotated reading | A conflict between scope and duration | Explain that the requested text and commentary may require several episodes; agree a sequence or an explicit scope change. |
| Mixed original reading and explanation | Which passages can be rephrased | Identify the sections or text types requiring original wording. |
| German text and an English listening preference | Language of the original and of commentary | Confirm whether to read the German and explain in English, or use another explicitly chosen arrangement. |
| Additional context requested but absent from sources | Permitted evidence for additions | Ask whether to use clearly identified external references; do not silently substitute model memory. |
| A scan or diagram cannot be read reliably | What can actually be covered accurately | Identify the affected material and the missing information. Do not ask preference questions to disguise an evidence gap. |
| Prior brief conflicts with new goals or sources | Which commitments still apply | Show the changed part and update it; do not carry forward an old scope automatically. |
| “Whatever is best” | Whether a recommendation has been accepted | Suggest a concrete treatment and explain its consequence, especially any omission or rewriting. |

Do not treat these as ten additional standard questions. They are examples of conditions that justify a follow-up. Ask the highest-consequence unresolved question next, and use its answer before choosing another.

## Three illustrative paths

### Annotated German literature

Suppose the learner says they must learn the supplied stories and concepts, wants the whole specified document covered, and chooses original reading with explanation. They know the broad period but struggle to understand why particular passages matter.

The source-treatment answer prevents the central error: producing an informative paraphrase. The useful remaining questions concern the actual reading scope, how source language and commentary language should work, and whether the requested context is available in the supplied notes. There is no reason to ask which narrative “hook” they prefer before those commitments are settled.

Possible closing brief, once those choices have actually been confirmed:

> We'll read all the material you identified for original reading, preserving its wording and sequence, with clearly signalled explanatory pauses. That includes the notes and concept sections if they are part of your agreed reading scope. Any sections assigned a different treatment will be named explicitly. We'll divide the material into episodes where needed to keep the requested text intact. Any unclear text or missing context will be identified before we claim the reading is complete.

This is a conditional illustration. The owner's actual document has not been supplied; its scope, language arrangement, and completeness have not been verified.

### Roman Empire requirements

Suppose the learner identifies a separate requirements list, wants to explain its ideas in their own words, and permits the episode to rephrase the source chapter. They find connections between political arrangements and everyday life difficult.

The resulting brief should name the requirements document and teach its actual explanatory demands. It should not reduce a requirement such as “explain” to mentioning a term once. Full reading of every source sentence would add a different obligation the learner has not requested.

Possible closing brief:

> We'll use the requirements list to establish what must be taught, and the chapter as supporting material. We'll explain the required ideas in our own words and spend extra time on the connections you've identified. We'll flag requirements the supplied material cannot support.

The real list is still needed to determine which explanations belong in the episode.

### Economics: knowing a term without knowing the calculation

Suppose the learner wants to answer numerical questions, identifies their exercise sheet, permits rephrasing, and says they know the definitions but cannot tell which quantities to use.

That is enough to propose help with reading the question, choosing a relationship, selecting quantities, performing the steps, and interpreting the answer. Do not spend the opening of the episode repeatedly defining the familiar term. Do not infer a formula from “GDP” alone: inspect the question and the course conventions.

Possible closing brief:

> We'll focus on how to start the kinds of calculations in your exercise sheet. We'll work through a representative example, explaining why each quantity and step belongs there. The spoken explanation will stand on its own, with a saved calculation available if you want to look. We'll offer another course question afterward that asks you to choose the method yourself, then check the choice, calculation, and interpretation separately.

The saved calculation and follow-on question are proposed support, not demonstrated learning benefits. The follow-on question needs reference guidance and comparable difficulty; merely changing the numbers is insufficient to assess broader method selection. One answer still provides limited evidence. A learner's preference for a continuous listening session should be respected; practice can be offered separately rather than inserted as compulsory interruptions.

## The final brief and stopping rule

End with a short, ordinary-language statement of the outcome, required material, source treatment, starting point, and help to provide. Include consequential language or listening choices and disclose any known gaps. Invite a correction to this concrete understanding before generation. This brief confirmation is part of the proposed learner experience; it is not a requirement for repeated approval of development work.

Preparation is sufficient when all five areas are addressed and no unresolved answer could materially change required coverage, original-word treatment, the target task, or support essential to following the episode. Missing learner knowledge can be the reason for teaching. Missing required source material limits what can be promised.

A learner can explicitly choose a narrower episode or accept a clearly described gap. Record the changed commitment. Do not quietly preserve a “complete course preparation” label after dropping material.

For a returning learner, the candidate proposal is to show the five existing answers, identify changes to sources and goals, and ask the learner to confirm or update the brief. This preserves required preparation while avoiding repeated transcription. Whether a combined confirmation reliably catches stale answers still needs observation with real learners.

## What the prototype still needs to establish

Scenario inspection can expose contradictions in these rules; it cannot show that learners understand this wording, volunteer accurate answers, or learn more from the resulting episode. The next evaluation should observe which questions change the brief, which answers are misunderstood, and whether the proposed support helps with the intended task. Revise the wording and follow-up conditions from those observations rather than treating this document as settled policy.

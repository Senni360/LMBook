# Learning evidence for preparation and audio teaching

Reviewed: 2026-09-14. Scope: a focused primary-source review for the [learning-experience research plan](learning-experience-plan.md), not a systematic review, production specification, or evaluation of LMBook learners. Active duration was not measured. No participant sessions, provider experiments, listening trials, or learning gains were produced by this work.

## Decision-level finding

The evidence supports investigating **a brief that identifies the learning task and the particular point of difficulty**, followed by teaching and evaluation matched to that task. It does not establish an optimal interview length, validate five questions, or show that longer preparation causes better learning. Five required areas remain the owner's working direction.

For economics, preserve the distinction between explaining a concept, identifying a suitable method, executing its steps, and interpreting an answer. Treat those as things to investigate, not permanent learner categories. A promising research candidate is an explained example, a selective opportunity to predict or explain a consequential step, and a new problem afterward. The balance needs testing: neither uninterrupted exposition nor compulsory questioning at every step is supported as a universal solution.

Do not assume walking makes meaningful learning impossible. Equally, do not infer from treadmill podcast findings that a listener can learn unfamiliar multistep calculations while navigating outdoors. Optional persistent visual support and later practice remain hypotheses to compare.

## Primary studies inspected

Each capsule separates the observed study from a proposed application. Publication year is shown; data-collection dates are supplied only where verified. “Full text inspected” means the methods, relevant results, and limitations were accessed; it does not mean data were reanalysed or the experiment replicated.

### E1. Conceptual and procedural knowledge develop together

Rittle-Johnson, Siegler, and Alibali (2001), *Developing conceptual understanding and procedural skill in mathematics: An iterative process*. [Author-hosted paper](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/2001-Siegler-Alibali.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.346).

**Access:** full PDF; methods, both experiments' results, and discussion inspected (journal pp. 349–360).

**Study and finding:** Experiments involved 74 fifth-graders and 117 fifth-/sixth-graders learning decimal number-line tasks. Initial conceptual knowledge predicted procedural improvement; procedural gains predicted conceptual improvement. Experiment 2 manipulated representational support and found effects on procedural learning that varied with prior knowledge. Some relationships were correlational; the experiment did not establish every arrow in the proposed developmental model.

**Limit and inference:** This is elementary mathematics, not economics or audio. It argues against equating familiar terminology with complete understanding, and against rigidly separating “theory learners” from “calculation learners.” Ask where the learner gets stuck and connect principles to operations while checking both.

### E2. A small performance sample can inform adaptation, but needs validation

Kalyuga and Sweller (2004), *Measuring knowledge to optimize cognitive load factors during instruction*. [Author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232596386_Measuring_Knowledge_to_Optimize_Cognitive_Load_Factors_During_Instruction), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.558).

**Access:** author-uploaded full-text transcription; methods/results, especially Experiments 3–4 and general discussion (pp. 562–566), inspected. The PDF linked by UNSW failed to load.

**Study and finding:** School mathematics experiments assessed knowledge through the next solution step. In Experiment 4, 26 Year 10/11 boys received performance-adapted algebra instruction or instruction yoked to another learner's route. The adapted group gained more on the rapid diagnostic measure. Earlier experiments compared rapid and conventional tests.

**Limit and inference:** Small, narrow samples; short tests had low internal reliability, and outcomes were task-specific. A course-based “how would you start?” question may identify useful support without a full exam. One answer cannot justify a broad mastery label, and an AI-generated probe would need its own correctness and diagnostic checks.

### E3. Worked examples can help acquisition; broad transfer is a separate question

Sweller and Cooper (1985), *The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra*. [Original paper hosted for a university teaching course](https://onderwijs.felienne.nl/vakdidactiek/materiaal/sweller_worked_examples.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3).

**Access:** full paper; Experiments 2–5 methods/results and general discussion inspected (pp. 69–89).

**Study and finding:** In Experiment 3, 22 Year 9 students studied examples alternating with problems or solved problems throughout. The example condition learned faster and subsequently solved structurally similar algebra problems faster with fewer errors. Experiment 2 had not shown a significant test-time difference. Later experiments examined structurally different problems; the benefits were substantially specific to similar structures.

**Limit and inference:** Small school samples and immediate algebra outcomes do not establish podcast learning or general calculation mastery. Show a method being applied, then assess a new problem. A polished recitation of the example is insufficient evidence of transfer; vary more than the numbers when that matches the course.

### E4. Self-explanation is not automatically better than another useful activity

Matthews and Rittle-Johnson (2009; available proof dated 2008), *In pursuit of knowledge: Comparing self-explanations, concepts, and procedures as pedagogical tools*. [Author-hosted proof](https://web.education.wisc.edu/pmatthews/wp-content/uploads/sites/35/2012/12/matthews-Rittle-Johnson-2009.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2008.08.004).

**Access:** full uncorrected proof; both experiments' methods/results and discussion inspected, particularly Experiment 2 (PDF pp. 13–19). Final typeset version not separately inspected.

**Study and finding:** Second- through fifth-graders learned mathematical equivalence. Experiment 1 (40 children) compared conceptual and procedural instruction with self-explanation. Experiment 2 (48 children) gave conceptual instruction to both groups; one explained, while the other solved extra problems to approximate equal study time. Added explanation prompts produced no detected advantage in procedural learning, transfer, or conceptual knowledge.

**Limit and inference:** This does not show that explaining is useless; the active comparator already offered conceptual instruction and practice. Require each explanation pause to serve a purpose. Compare it with spending that time on another example or practice rather than with doing nothing.

### E5. Recent self-explanation evidence also shows strategy interactions and failures

McCarthy and Hinze (2025), *Stage 2 Registered Report—You've got some explaining to do: Effects of explanation prompts on science text comprehension*. Published 28 July 2025. [Publisher full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.70093).

**Access:** publisher full text, including methods, manipulation checks, focal analyses, and discussion (§§5–7). Public data link located but data not reanalysed.

**Study and finding:** The paper reports 173 participants, mean age about 21, reading two science passages. It crossed typed self-explanation versus think-aloud with rereading, recall, or explanatory retrieval; comprehension was assessed seven days later. Self-explanation's benefit depended on subsequent activity. There was no overall offline-strategy benefit, and explanatory retrieval prompts did not improve retrieval quality.

**Limit and inference:** No silent-reading control; missing responses to one item were imputed. Neither audio nor calculations were tested. This supports investigating selective explanation while challenging the assumption that combining more named learning strategies must improve the result. Whether the learner actually performs the intended mental activity matters.

### E6. Retrieval can support inference, but cued prose transfer is not all transfer

Butler (2010), *Repeated testing produces superior transfer of learning relative to repeated studying*. [Full original paper, independent mirror](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf), [author's institutional record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/repeated-testing-produces-superior-transfer-of-learning-relative-/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019902).

**Access:** full PDF; all four experiments' methods/results and transfer-task construction inspected (pp. 1120–1130). Institutional record verifies bibliographic identity.

**Study and finding:** Four experiments used Washington University undergraduates (24, 24, 24, and 20 participants). Repeated short-answer tests with feedback after prose study improved one-week retention and new inferential answers relative to restudy. In the cross-domain experiment, questions explicitly cued the relevant concept and participants were told to apply prior learning.

**Limit and inference:** These outcomes do not establish spontaneous recognition of the right economic method in an unfamiliar question. Include an actual retrieval opportunity and feedback when appropriate, but assess method selection separately. Later questions should not inadvertently reveal the method whose selection is being evaluated.

### E7. Whole-problem testing after examples has yielded uncertain advantages

Van Gog, Kester, Dirkx, Hoogerheide, Boerboom, and Verkoeijen (2015), *Testing after worked example study does not enhance delayed problem-solving performance compared to restudy*. [Open full paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9297-3).

**Access:** full publisher HTML; four experiments' designs, delayed outcomes, within-paper pooled analysis, and discussion inspected.

**Study and finding:** University and Dutch vocational students studied electrical-circuit or probability examples. Four experiments varied example restudy, problem solving, or recall, with one-week tests. No individual experiment established a testing advantage. The pooled testing-minus-restudy estimate was +5.84 percentage points, with a 95% interval from −0.62 to +12.29; thus the results do not establish zero benefit.

**Limit and inference:** Some groups were small; practice was relatively brief and differed from repeated retrieval with feedback. This is a boundary case for transferring generic retrieval advice to numerical skills. Test whether selective practice adds value at the learner's current stage rather than treating a difficult full problem as automatically productive.

### E8. Predicting individual steps is promising, with a cost and selective participation

Van den Broek, Van Wermeskerken, and Van Gog (2025), *Retrieval practice in stepwise worked examples improves learning*. [Utrecht-hosted full paper](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/269108220/1-s2.0-S0959475225001203-main.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102196).

**Access:** full PDF; methods, main/exploratory results, and limitations inspected (§§2–4). Journal issue December 2025, preceding this review date.

**Study and finding:** Of 240 randomized online adults aged 18–25, 164 supplied included delayed data. Displayed genetics examples incorporated spoken predictions of upcoming steps or no prediction prompts. One-week procedural recall improved; the problem-solving advantage was small (r=.18, p=.048). Average example-study time was approximately 31 versus 13 minutes. Attrition was higher with prompts.

**Limit and inference:** Analyses cannot fully remove unequal participation or time differences. Examples remained visible; this was not audio-only instruction. Trial a few predictions at consequential steps. Do not assume people silently listening will enact the intervention, or require constant spoken responses without assessing burden and completion.

### E9. Segmenting speech can help; the study does not prescribe podcast lengths

Singh, Marcus, and Ayres (2012), *The transient information effect: Investigating the impact of segmentation on spoken and written text*. [Author-uploaded full paper](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Nadine-Marcus/publication/264681373_The_Transient_Information_Effect_Investigating_the_Impact_of_Segmentation_on_Spoken_and_Written_text/links/5a711ad9458515015e64051d/The-Transient-Information-Effect-Investigating-the-Impact-of-Segmentation-on-Spoken-and-Written-text.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2885).

**Access:** full six-page paper; both experiments' methods, contrasts, and discussion inspected (pp. 849–853).

**Study and finding:** Two randomized experiments involved 63 and 64 Sydney Grade 10 commerce students learning how legislation passes. A 108-second spoken passage was continuous or broken into eight parts, with equal total pause time. Segmentation benefited spoken-text test performance; transfer benefit appeared in Experiment 2, not Experiment 1. Allowing rereading also affected modality comparisons.

**Limit and inference:** Brief classroom procedural exposition is not a lengthy conversational lesson. Compare pauses at meaningful boundaries and convenient replay. A persistent equation or graph is a candidate aid when earlier quantities must be consulted; these experiments did not test that aid or establish a universal optimal segment duration.

### E10. Actual treadmill podcast evidence argues against dismissing walking

Wolpaw and colleagues (2022), *A multimodal evaluation of podcast learning, retention, and electroencephalographically measured attention in medical trainees*. Published 9 November 2022; data collected January–April 2021. [Original paper in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9733582/), [full PDF mirror inspected](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/688a/0abba0b5ce2ed34752de54cca91e29211897.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.31289).

**Access:** full ten-page PDF, including methods, topic-specific results, and limitations. PMC intermittently returned a CAPTCHA.

**Study and finding:** A randomized repeated-measures comparison involved 61 medical trainees completing approximately 45-minute podcasts seated/on a treadmill and textbook reading across three topics. Podcasts improved immediate scores relative to reading in two topics. Statistics scores showed no condition difference; gains persisted four weeks. Outcomes used multiple-choice tests.

**Limit and inference:** No equivalence design, outdoor navigation, phone visuals, or independent economics calculations. The statistics interaction sentence contains inconsistent wording/p-value, so it should not anchor a precise effect claim. Walking is a credible learning context to evaluate; this study neither proves equivalence in general nor supports banning demanding content from walks.

## Leads with limited access or deliberately limited use

- **Leahy and Sweller (2011), [modality and transient information](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1787): abstract assessed; full text unavailable in this pass.** Two primary-school experiments (N=24 and N=64) compared narrated versus written graph instructions; the abstract reports opposite modality results with longer versus shortened explanations. Useful lead against “audio plus picture is always better,” but methods/confounds were not fully assessed. E9 supplies the inspected primary evidence for segmentation. [Author listing/abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230074023_Cognitive_load_theory_modality_of_presentation_and_the_transient_information_effect).
- **Chi and colleagues (1989), [self-explanation while studying examples](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-bassok-m-lewis-m-reimann-p-glaser-r-1989-self-explanations-how-students): author PDF located, but scanned pages did not produce inspectable text in the web tool.** It was not used as a fully assessed causal experiment. E4 and E5 provide directly inspected evidence and counterevidence for explanation prompts.
- **Chi and colleagues (1994), [elicited self-explanations](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0364021394900167): abstract inspected; author full-text link failed.** No additional claims here depend on this study.
- **Helfer and colleagues (2023), [walking, listening, and remembering](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10713016/): abstract and article page screened, detailed access interrupted by CAPTCHA.** Not used to infer educational gains or specify a walking limit. Word-memory, gait, and speech-perception studies are not substitutes for numerical-learning outcomes.

## Implications to carry into research prototypes

These are design inferences, not experimentally established LMBook behavior.

| Interview or lesson choice | Rationale and boundary | Observable check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask what a numerical question requires and where the learner stops | E1–E2 distinguish related knowledge and task-level performance. Do not infer mastery from a familiar term or one correct answer. | Does the brief identify a need that changes the example or explanation? |
| Offer a small course-based starting-step probe when an answer remains ambiguous | A candidate diagnostic activity, not an entrance exam or validated instrument. | Does it correctly distinguish missing method selection from missing arithmetic, with a teacher/source reference? |
| Explain an example, then selectively ask for a prediction or reason | E3–E8 support comparing this with sustained explanation and useful alternative practice. | New-question performance, incorrect steps, effort, and whether learners actually attempt the pause. |
| Make quantities and relationships inspectable where needed | E9 motivates reducing dependence on remembered earlier speech; it does not prove animations outperform stills. | Can the listener continue without repeatedly losing numbers? Does looking help enough to justify interruption? |
| Preserve walking as a primary listening context | E10 challenges a categorical dual-task objection. | Compare a familiar walk with seated listening using matched material, and record environmental interruptions. |
| Separate immediate following from later use | E3, E6–E8 motivate independent and delayed tasks. | Method selection, execution, units, and interpretation on a different problem; later interval and other study recorded. |

## Counterarguments and unresolved boundaries

1. **Preparation and teaching are different interventions.** Evidence for a useful teaching activity does not show that an interview can infer who needs it, or that five questions will do so. Evaluate brief correctness and subsequent learning separately.
2. **“I understand the theory” is neither proof nor something to dismiss.** It is the learner's useful report. A representative question can reveal a narrower difficulty, while the response may also depend on wording, anxiety, language, or missing course conventions.
3. **Effort is not a benefit by itself.** More questioning can displace another productive activity. A harder condition can also lose participants; examining only completers could mislead us about regular use.
4. **No inspected study established the best phone visual for this use case.** Compare audio alone, a persistent worked calculation/still graph, and a staged visual only when each can address an observed difficulty. Do not make animation an assumed requirement.
5. **Task-specific success needs appropriate material.** GDP and unemployment topic labels do not specify which quantity, method, population convention, or assessment skill is required. The owner's actual course questions are still needed before validating diagnostic items or scoring calculation outcomes.
6. **The most consequential evidence gap is ecological.** No inspected experiment combines LMBook-style preparation, the owner's course material, natural two-person teaching, outdoor walking, optional phone visuals, and independently assessed calculations. An exploratory owner/friends pilot should investigate that combination without claiming a general effect size.
7. **This review does not determine politics/history/biology curricula or source fidelity.** The annotated German reading and requirements-based history experience remain distinct user needs; learning-strategy studies cannot override those commitments. Biology's eventual scope still requires its learners' perspectives.

## Next investigation that can change the decision

Use one representative course question and its marking guidance to check whether the candidate interview identifies the missing operation, rather than merely producing a plausible learner profile. Then compare a bounded example with and without selected prediction/explanation opportunities, recording both willingness to engage and an independently scored new problem. Actual learner materials and participation are dependencies for that conclusion, not reasons to delay independent conversation-prototype work.

Do not infer a percentage improvement, an optimal preparation duration, or validated readiness from this memo. The reviewed studies make the proposed comparisons more defensible; they do not supply their results.
